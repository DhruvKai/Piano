# Remembering Chord Fingering

Practical memory tricks for [[Chord]] fingering — RH `1-3-5`, LH `5-3-1` — and why it's built the way it is.

## The hand shape barely changes between chords
RH `1-3-5` = thumb, middle finger, pinky — skip the index (2) and ring (4) entirely. That shape spans a fixed distance (a 5th) every time, so once your hand learns it once, it works for **every** major or minor chord — you're just sliding the same shape to a different starting key, the same way [[Major Scale]] is one formula moved to different roots.

## Where the half steps are (major vs minor)
A triad = root + 3rd + 5th. Counting in [[Sharps & Flats|half steps]] from the root:

| Triad | Root → 3rd (finger 1→3) | 3rd → 5th (finger 3→5) |
|---|---|---|
| Major | 4 half steps | 3 half steps |
| Minor | 3 half steps | 4 half steps |

Both add up to 7 half steps root-to-5th either way — **only the middle finger's target changes.**

![[chord-shape-major-vs-minor.svg]]

This is the single most useful shortcut for chord fingering: **major → minor is "move finger 3 down one key."** Root (finger 1) and 5th (finger 5) never move.

## Practical tips
- **Start with the all-white-key majors**: C, F, and G major use only white keys — the easiest hand shape to feel out first, no black-key stretches to think about.
- **Practice the shape off the keys first**: rest your hand in the `1-3-5` claw shape on a flat surface before trying it on the piano, so your fingers learn the *spacing* independent of hunting for the right keys.
- **Say the notes while placing fingers**: "thumb-C, middle-E, pinky-G" out loud links the finger number to the note, not just the shape.
- **Watch for the outlier**: this course's B major chord breaks the pattern (RH `1-2-3` instead of `1-3-5`) — see [[Lesson 05 - Chords]]. Knowing it's the one exception makes it easier to remember, not harder.
- **Use a [[Metronome]]**: switch between a chord's major and minor version in time — it drills the "only finger 3 moves" habit until it's automatic.
