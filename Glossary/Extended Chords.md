# Extended Chords (7ths, 9ths, 11ths, 13ths)

An **extended chord** is a [[Chord|triad]] with more notes stacked on top. You keep going up the same way the triad was built (every other note of the scale) past the 7th, into the 9th, 11th and 13th. They're what makes a chord sound "jazzy", "lush", "soulful" or "expensive" instead of plain.

> [!info] Source
> General music theory, not a PIX Series lesson. It builds directly on [[Chord]], [[Major Scale]], [[Minor Scale]] and [[Remembering Chord Notes]]. Every chord is spelled using this vault's sharp-only keyboard names, and the 12-key table and the diagrams were generated by script (`Scripts/gen_extended_chord_svgs.py`) rather than typed by hand.

> [!tip] The whole topic in four lines
> 1. **Triad** = R 3 5. Add a **7th** → a 7th chord (the foundation of everything here).
> 2. Keep stacking thirds: **9** = the 2nd, **11** = the 4th, **13** = the 6th, all an octave up.
> 3. The **number in the name is the highest extension** and (unless it says *add*) implies the 7th underneath it.
> 4. On piano you almost **never play every note**: keep the 3rd, 7th and the top extension; drop the 5th first.

## Building chords by stacking thirds

A [[Chord|triad]] is built by taking the root, skipping a note, taking the next, skipping, taking the next: on white keys from C that's **C E G**. Extended chords just *keep going*:

![[Assets/Glossary/Extended Chords/extended-stacking-thirds.svg|440]]

| Notes stacked | Name | Notes on C | Extra note |
|---|---|---|---|
| 3 | Triad | C E G | — |
| 4 | 7th chord | C E G **B** | 7th |
| 5 | 9th chord | C E G B **D** | 9th |
| 6 | 11th chord | C E G B D **F** | 11th |
| 7 | 13th chord | C E G B D F **A** | 13th |

After the 13th the next third is **C** again, the root two octaves up. So the 13th chord contains all 7 notes of the scale and **nothing can extend further**. That's why the list stops at 13.

## Why 9, 11 and 13

Keep counting up the [[Major Scale]] past the [[Octave]] instead of starting again at 1:

![[Assets/Glossary/Extended Chords/extended-degree-numbers.svg|440]]

| Extension | Same letter as | On C | Semitones above root | Key count (root = 1) |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **9** | 2 | D | 14 | 15 |
| **11** | 4 | F | 17 | 18 |
| **13** | 6 | A | 21 | 22 |

The rule: **extension − 7 = the scale degree** (9 − 7 = 2, 11 − 7 = 4, 13 − 7 = 6).

> [!question] So why say "9" instead of "2"?
> Because it tells you **the 7th is there too**. "C2" or "Cadd2" would mean "a C triad plus D". "C9" means "a C 7th chord *and then* a D on top". It's the same D key, but the chord sounds different. Even numbers (8, 10, 12, 14) never show up because they're just the root, 3rd, 5th and 7th again an octave higher, so nothing new is added.

> [!info] The "key count" column
> It's the [[Remembering Chord Notes]] trick extended: put the root on "1" and count **every** key, white and black. Major triad = 1-5-8; a 9th lands on 15, an 11th on 18, a 13th on 22. In practice it's faster to think *"9 = one whole step above the root, an octave up"*, *"11 = the 4th"*, *"13 = the 6th"*.

## Step 1 — 7th chords

Every 9th, 11th and 13th chord is a **7th chord with extra notes on top**, so learn these first. A 7th chord = triad + a 7th. There are **two 7ths** you can add:

- **Major 7th (7)**: 11 semitones above the root, one key **below** the octave. On C: **B**.
- **Minor / flat 7th (♭7)**: 10 semitones above the root, two keys below the octave. On C: **A#**.

Combine those with a major, minor or diminished triad and you get the five everyday 7th chords plus one rarer one:

| Chord | Symbol on C | Formula | Semitones from root | Key count | Notes on C | Sound |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Major 7th** | Cmaj7 | R 3 5 7 | 0 4 7 11 | 1-5-8-12 | C E G B | dreamy, soft, lush |
| **Dominant 7th** | C7 | R 3 5 ♭7 | 0 4 7 10 | 1-5-8-11 | C E G A# | bluesy, restless, wants to resolve |
| **Minor 7th** | Cm7 | R ♭3 5 ♭7 | 0 3 7 10 | 1-4-8-11 | C D# G A# | mellow, smooth, soulful |
| **Half-diminished** | Cm7♭5 / Cø | R ♭3 ♭5 ♭7 | 0 3 6 10 | 1-4-7-11 | C D# F# A# | dark, unresolved, searching |
| **Diminished 7th** | Cdim7 / C°7 | R ♭3 ♭5 ♭♭7 | 0 3 6 9 | 1-4-7-10 | C D# F# A | tense, dramatic, spooky |
| **Minor-major 7th** | Cm(maj7) | R ♭3 5 7 | 0 3 7 11 | 1-4-8-12 | C D# G B | mysterious, "spy film" |

> [!tip] The 7th-chord ladder — one key moves at a time
> Start on Cmaj7 and lower **one** note by a semitone each step:
> **Cmaj7** C E G B → (B→A#) **C7** C E G A# → (E→D#) **Cm7** C D# G A# → (G→F#) **Cm7♭5** C D# F# A# → (A#→A) **Cdim7** C D# F# A.
> Play the ladder up and down on every root. It's the fastest way to hear and feel what each chord is.

> [!warning] C7 is **not** "C major 7"
> A plain **7** means the **flat** 7th (dominant). Only **maj7** gets the major 7th: **C7 = C E G A#**, **Cmaj7 = C E G B**. This is the #1 beginner mix-up, and it carries into every extension (C9 vs Cmaj9, C13 vs Cmaj13).

**Fingering:** root-position 7th chords are usually **RH 1-2-3-5** and **LH 5-3-2-1** (see [[Fingering Notation]]). The 4th note pushes finger 2 or 4 into the shape that a triad's 1-3-5 skips.

**Fun fact:** a dim7 is just minor 3rds stacked all the way round (3+3+3, then 3 more back to the root), so it's **symmetrical**. Cdim7, D#dim7, F#dim7 and Adim7 are the same four keys. There are only **3 different dim7 chords** in all of music.

## Reading chord symbols

| Rule | Example | Means |
|---|---|---|
| A number on its own (7, 9, 11, 13) = **dominant**: ♭7 included | **C9** | C E G **A#** D |
| **maj** only changes the **7th**, never the extensions | **Cmaj9** | C E G **B** D |
| **m** only changes the **3rd** | **Cm9** | C **D#** G A# D |
| **add** = add that note, **no 7th** | **Cadd9** | C E G D |
| **6** = add the 6th, no 7th | **C6** · **C6/9** | C E G A · C E G A D |
| **sus** = **replace** the 3rd with the 2nd or 4th | **Csus4** · **C9sus4** | C F G · C F G A# D |
| Alterations come after the number | **C7♭9** · **C7#11** | C E G A# **C#** · C E G A# D **F#** |
| A slash = which note is in the **bass** | **A#/C** | A# D F with C in the bass (≈ C11 without its 3rd) |

**Symbol synonyms** you'll see on real chord charts:

| Chord | Written as |
|---|---|
| Major 7th | Cmaj7 · CM7 · CΔ7 · CΔ |
| Dominant 7th | C7 · Cdom7 |
| Minor 7th | Cm7 · Cmin7 · C-7 |
| Half-diminished | Cm7♭5 · Cø · Cø7 |
| Diminished 7th | Cdim7 · C°7 |
| Minor-major 7th | Cm(maj7) · CmM7 · C-Δ7 |
| Augmented | Caug · C+ (C E G#) |

> [!info] Why chord symbols still say ♭
> Chord names describe **degrees**, and sheet music writes them as ♭7, ♭9, ♭13, so this note keeps those names (m7♭5, 7♭9) to match what you'll read on real charts. The **keys themselves** are always spelled sharp-only, like everywhere in this vault: the ♭7 of C is written **A#**. A formal theory book would call it B♭, but it's the same physical key (see [[Sharps & Flats]]).

## 9th chords

Add the **9** (the 2nd, an octave up) on top of a 7th chord. Ninths are the most common extension by far: warm and rich without sounding complicated.

| Chord | Formula | Notes on C | Usually played | Character |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Cadd9** | R 3 5 9 (no 7th) | C E G D | all | open, pop/rock, "sparkly" major |
| **Cm(add9)** | R ♭3 5 9 (no 7th) | C D# G D | all | sad but pretty, ballad-ish |
| **Cmaj9** | R 3 5 7 9 | C E G B D | C E B D | dreamy, R&B / neo-soul staple |
| **C9** | R 3 5 ♭7 9 | C E G A# D | C E A# D | funky, bluesy, gospel |
| **Cm9** | R ♭3 5 ♭7 9 | C D# G A# D | C D# A# D | smooth, lo-fi, soulful minor |

| **Cm9** | ![[Assets/Glossary/Extended Chords/chord-c-m9.svg\|440]] |

> [!warning] add9 vs 9
> **Cadd9 has no 7th; C9 does.** They sound noticeably different: add9 is bright and simple, 9 is bluesy and wants to move on. If a song chart says *Cadd9* and you play C9, it will sound wrong.

> [!tip] Shortcut: build a 9th chord from a 7th chord on the **3rd**
> The top four notes of any 9th chord (3 5 7 9) form another, simpler 7th chord:
> - **Cmaj9** top = E G B D = **Em7** → play Em7 in the RH over C in the LH
> - **Cm9** top = D# G A# D = **D#maj7** over C
> - **C9** top = E G A# D = **Em7♭5** over C
> So "a 7th chord a 3rd up, with the root in the bass" gives you a 9th chord.

## 11th chords

Add the **11** (the 4th, an octave up) on top of a 9th chord. This is where the **clash problem** shows up:

> [!warning] The 11 vs the major 3rd
> On C the 11 is **F**, and the major 3rd is **E**, only one semitone below it. A major 3rd with a natural 11 above it sounds harsh and muddy. So:
> - **Dominant 11 (C11)** → **drop the 3rd**. The result sounds like a sus chord (it really is C9sus4).
> - **Major chords** → raise the 11 to **#11** (F#): **Cmaj7#11**. That's the bright, floating "Lydian" sound.
> - **Minor chords** have no clash (the ♭3 D# is a whole tone below F), so **Cm11** works with everything in. It's the most usable 11th chord.

| Chord | Formula | Notes on C | Usually played | Character |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **C11** | R (3) 5 ♭7 9 11 | C E G A# D F | C A# D F (= A#/C) | open, suspended, gospel/funk |
| **Cm11** | R ♭3 5 ♭7 9 11 | C D# G A# D F | C D# A# D F | lush, modal, neo-soul minor |
| **Cmaj7#11** | R 3 5 7 9 #11 | C E G B D F# | C E B D F# | floating, film-score, "Lydian" |
| **C7#11** | R 3 5 ♭7 9 #11 | C E G A# D F# | C E A# D F# | bright dominant, jazz/bossa |

| **Cm11** | ![[Assets/Glossary/Extended Chords/chord-c-m11.svg\|440]] |

> [!tip] Slash-chord shortcut for C11
> Play an **A# major triad** (A# D F) in the RH over **C** in the LH. That's ♭7 9 11 over the root: an instant C11. The same trick works anywhere: the major triad a whole step below the root, over the root (e.g. **F/G** = G11, a very common gospel/pop sound).

## 13th chords

Add the **13** (the 6th, an octave up). In theory a 13th chord holds all 7 notes; on a piano you play maybe 4 or 5 of them.

| Chord | Formula | Notes on C | Usually played | Character |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **C13** | R 3 (5) ♭7 9 (11) 13 | C E G A# D F A | C E A# A (+ D) | big-band, gospel, "classic jazz" dominant |
| **Cmaj13** | R 3 (5) 7 9 (11) 13 | C E G B D F A | C E B D A | sweet, wide, very lush |
| **Cm13** | R ♭3 (5) ♭7 9 11 13 | C D# G A# D F A | C D# A# D A | dark-sweet, Dorian, neo-soul |

| Chord | Diagram |
|---|---|
| **C13** | ![[Assets/Glossary/Extended Chords/chord-c-13.svg\|440]] |
| **Cmaj13** | ![[Assets/Glossary/Extended Chords/chord-c-maj13.svg\|440]] |

On major and dominant 13ths the **11 is always left out** (same E–F clash as above), and the **5th** goes too. The defining sound of a dominant 13 is just **3, ♭7 and 13** (E, A#, A on C). Those three notes over a C bass already *are* C13.

## Altered extensions

On **dominant 7th chords** (the chord that wants to resolve: the V chord of a key) the 9th, 11th and 13th are often pushed a semitone up or down to add tension before resolving. These are called **altered** tensions:

| Alteration | On C | Semitones | Chord | Notes on C | Sound |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| **♭9** | C# | 13 | **C7♭9** | C E G A# C# | dark, Spanish/classical, minor-key V |
| **#9** | D# | 15 | **C7#9** | C E G A# D# | gritty, rock/funk ("Hendrix chord") |
| **#11** | F# | 18 | **C7#11** | C E G A# D F# | bright, jazzy (see [[#11th chords]]) |
| **♭13** | G# | 20 | **C7♭13** | C E G A# G# | moody, pulls hard toward a minor chord |

| Chord | Diagram |
|---|---|
| **C7♭9** | ![[Assets/Glossary/Extended Chords/chord-c-7b9.svg\|440]] |
| **C7#9** | ![[Assets/Glossary/Extended Chords/chord-c-7-sharp9.svg\|440]] |
| **C7♭13** | ![[Assets/Glossary/Extended Chords/chord-c-7b13.svg\|440]] |

- **#9 sits right next to the 3rd.** C7#9 has both D# and E, a "minor and major at once" crunch. It's nicknamed the *Hendrix chord* because Jimi Hendrix's *Purple Haze* leans on E7#9.
- **"7alt"** (C7alt) on a chart means *a dominant 7th with any mix of ♭9, #9, #11/♭5, ♭13/#5*. The player picks. It's a jazz-level topic; know that it exists and treat it as "C7 plus spicy notes".
- Altered tensions are **mainly for dominant chords** heading somewhere (V → I). On a maj7 or m7 chord they usually just sound wrong.

## 6, add and sus chords

Not extended chords in the strict sense (no 7th underneath), but they're used the same way, to colour a plain triad, and they're easy to confuse with the real thing.

| Chord | Formula | Notes on C | Note |
|---|---|---|---|
| **C6** | R 3 5 6 | C E G A | softer, older-style alternative to Cmaj7 (same 4 notes as Am7!) |
| **Cm6** | R ♭3 5 6 | C D# G A | bittersweet, jazz-minor tonic |
| **C6/9** | R 3 5 6 9 | C E G A D | the "final chord" of countless jazz/bossa tunes |
| **Csus2** | R 2 5 | C D G | open, no 3rd = neither major nor minor |
| **Csus4** | R 4 5 | C F G | tense, wants to fall back to C major |
| **C7sus4** | R 4 5 ♭7 | C F G A# | the dominant-sus sound (cousin of C11 / C9sus4) |

## What to leave out

You have 10 fingers, and a 13th chord crammed into one hand sounds like mud. When there are more notes than you can (or should) play, keep them in this order of importance:

1. **The 3rd**: decides major vs minor. Keep it (except in sus/11 chords, where it's dropped on purpose).
2. **The 7th**: decides maj7 vs dominant vs m7. Keep it.
3. **The highest extension**: the 9, 11 or 13 that gives the chord its name. Keep it, otherwise it isn't that chord.
4. **The root**: important, but the **left hand or a bass player** usually covers it, so the RH can skip it.
5. **The 5th**: adds almost nothing to the colour. **Drop it first.** (Keep it only if it's altered, ♭5 or #5.)
6. **Lower extensions**: in a 13th chord the 9 is optional and the 11 is out (major/dominant).

The 3rd and 7th together are called the **guide tones**, the two notes that "say" what the chord is. A whole chord can be played as just **root (LH) + 3 + 7 + one extension (RH)**.

## How to play them on piano

Four standard approaches, from easiest to most advanced:

| Approach | LH | RH | Example: Cmaj9 | When |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **1. Root + full chord** | root (or root + 5th) | the rest, close together | LH C · RH E G B D | the default; easiest to read |
| **2. Shell voicing** | root + 7th (or root + 3rd) | 3rd/7th + extensions | LH C B · RH E G D | fuller sound, frees the RH for melody |
| **3. Rootless voicing** | root | 4 notes, **no root**: 3-5-7-9 or 7-9-3-5 | LH C · RH E G B D | jazz / neo-soul standard (worked example below) |
| **4. Spread / open voicing** | root + 5th or root + 10th | wide extensions on top | LH C G · RH B D E | ballads, big sound |

**Rules of thumb**
- **Keep 3rds and 7ths out of the low bass.** Below roughly the C an octave under [[Middle C]], close intervals turn to mud. The LH low notes should be the root, the 5th or an octave.
- **Put the colour on top.** The extension (9, 11, 13) usually sounds best as the RH's top or second-from-top note.
- **RH fingering for 4-note voicings:** **1-2-3-5** or **1-2-4-5**, low to high, whichever fits the gaps. 5-note chords use all five.
- **Move as little as possible between chords** (*voice leading*). Good extended voicings keep common notes and slide the rest by a step, as below.

### Worked example — ii–V–I in C

The **ii–V–I** (Dm → G → C in the key of C) is the most important progression in jazz and shows up constantly in pop, R&B and gospel. Upgraded to extended chords it becomes **Dm9 – G13 – Cmaj9**, played with LH roots and RH rootless voicings:

| Chord | LH | RH (low → high) | Degrees in RH | What moves |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Dm9** | D | F A C E | ♭3 5 ♭7 9 | — |
| **G13** | G | F A B E | ♭7 9 3 13 | only **C → B** |
| **Cmaj9** | C | E G B D | 3 5 7 9 | every note slides **down** to the next chord tone |

From Dm9 to G13 your RH moves **one finger by one key**, yet the harmony changes completely. That's the payoff of extended chords: richer sound with *less* movement than plain triads.

## Extended chords inside a key

Stack thirds on every note of the C major scale, using only white keys, and you get the **diatonic** 7th chords. The same seven roots (C to B) as [[Lesson 07 - Basic Arpeggios]] and [[Lesson 08 - Advanced Arpeggios]], now with 7ths:

| Degree | 7th chord | Notes | 9 | 11 | 13 | Extended chords that sound good |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| I | **Cmaj7** | C E G B | 9 (D) | 11 (F) ⚠️ | 13 (A) | Cmaj9, Cmaj13, C6/9 |
| ii | **Dm7** | D F A C | 9 (E) | 11 (G) | 13 (B) | Dm9, Dm11, Dm13 — all safe |
| iii | **Em7** | E G B D | ♭9 (F) ⚠️ | 11 (A) | ♭13 (C) ⚠️ | Em7, Em11 (skip the 9) |
| IV | **Fmaj7** | F A C E | 9 (G) | #11 (B) | 13 (D) | Fmaj9, Fmaj7#11, Fmaj13 — all safe |
| V | **G7** | G B D F | 9 (A) | 11 (C) ⚠️ | 13 (E) | G9, G13, G9sus4 |
| vi | **Am7** | A C E G | 9 (B) | 11 (D) | ♭13 (F) ⚠️ | Am9, Am11 |
| vii | **Bm7♭5** | B D F A | ♭9 (C) ⚠️ | 11 (E) | ♭13 (G) | Bm7♭5, Bm7♭5(11) |

⚠️ = an **avoid note**, a key that sits a semitone above a chord tone (C over B, F over E) and clashes. The simple rule: **an extension one semitone above a chord tone usually sounds wrong**; leave it out, or use it only as a passing note in a melody.

The same table works in every major key: the pattern **maj7 – m7 – m7 – maj7 – 7 – m7 – m7♭5** never changes, just like the [[Major Scale]] formula.

## Progressions to practise

All in the key of C, using white keys and the [[Gold Silver Bronze Chords|Gold-tier]] roots wherever possible:

| Style | Plain version | Extended version |
|---|---|---|
| Jazz ii–V–I | Dm – G – C | **Dm9 – G13 – Cmaj9** |
| Pop / ballad | C – G – Am – F | **Cadd9 – G – Am7 – Fmaj7** |
| Neo-soul / lo-fi | F – Em – Dm – C | **Fmaj9 – Em7 – Dm9 – Cmaj9** |
| I–vi–ii–V turnaround | C – Am – Dm – G | **Cmaj7 – Am7 – Dm7 – G7** → then **Cmaj9 – Am9 – Dm9 – G13** |
| 12-bar blues | C – F – G | **C9 – F9 – G13** (every chord a dominant) |
| Minor ii–V–i (C minor) | Ddim – G – Cm | **Dm7♭5 – G7♭9 – Cm9** = D F G# C → G B D F G# → C D# G A# D |

## Common mistakes

- **Playing C7 when the chart says Cmaj7** (or vice versa). Plain 7 = ♭7. See [[#Step 1 — 7th chords]].
- **Treating add9 and 9 as the same chord.** add9 has no 7th.
- **Natural 11 over a major 3rd.** Use #11 on major chords, drop the 3rd on dominant 11s, keep it freely on minor chords.
- **Trying to play every note.** A 13th chord needs 3 or 4 well-chosen notes, not 7. See [[#What to leave out]].
- **Muddy low voicings.** Keep the LH to root/5th/octave down low; put 3rds, 7ths and extensions in the middle of the keyboard.
- **Doubling the root in both hands.** If the LH has the root, the RH usually shouldn't. Spend that finger on a colour tone.
- **Spelling confusion with outside charts.** Real charts write ♭ names (B♭, E♭); this vault writes the same keys as A#, D#. F7 here is F A C D#, which a chart would print as F A C E♭.

## Practice plan

> [!tip] Practise with a [[Metronome]]
> Slowly, one chord per 4 beats. Say the chord name and its degrees out loud while you play it. The point is to hear the colour of each extension, not just find the keys.

- [ ] Play the **7th-chord ladder** (Cmaj7 → C7 → Cm7 → Cm7♭5 → Cdim7) on C, then on F and G
- [ ] Play the 7 **diatonic 7th chords** of C (Cmaj7 Dm7 Em7 Fmaj7 G7 Am7 Bm7♭5), RH 1-2-3-5
- [ ] Add the 9: Cmaj9, Dm9, Fmaj9, G9, Am9 (skip Em and Bm7♭5, see the avoid notes)
- [ ] Learn add9 vs 9 by ear: Cadd9 ↔ C9, back and forth
- [ ] Play the **ii–V–I** Dm9 – G13 – Cmaj9 with the rootless voicings above until the RH moves feel automatic
- [ ] Same ii–V–I in **F** (Gm9 – C13 – Fmaj9) and **G** (Am9 – D13 – Gmaj9)
- [ ] Try the slash-chord shortcuts: Em7/C = Cmaj9, A#/C = C11, F/G = G11
- [ ] Play each progression in [[#Progressions to practise]]
- [ ] Work through the [[#All 12 keys]] table, one root per day
